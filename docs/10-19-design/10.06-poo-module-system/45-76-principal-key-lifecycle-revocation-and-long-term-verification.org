#+title: Principal and Key Lifecycle, Revocation, and Long-Term Verification
#+startup: overview

* Status

This RFC records the accepted cryptographic identity, key rotation, revocation,
algorithm migration, and long-term verification semantics for POO Flow.

It extends governance decisions, distributed cache provenance, evidence
retention, temporal observations, and destructive erasure.

* Typst formalization: separated principal and key identities

Principal identity, role authority, signing key, encryption key, and trust
anchor are separate objects.  A valid key is usable only through an exact
purpose-, scope-, algorithm-, time-, and trust-bound key binding; possession
alone never grants governance or action authority.

#+begin_src typst
#let key-use-checks(binding, use, current) = (
  binding.key == use.key
  and binding.purpose == use.purpose
  and binding.scope == use.scope
  and valid-at(binding, current.time-observation)
  and not revoked-at(binding.key, current.time-observation)
  and algorithm-allowed(binding.algorithm, current.policy)
  and trust-path-valid(binding, current.trust-anchors)
)

$ possess(active-key)
  ==> not governance-authority(active-key)
  and not action-authority(active-key) $

$ authorize-new-signature(use)
  ==> key-use-checks(use.binding, use, current) $

$ revoked(key) or expired(key)
  ==> not authorize-new-signature(key) $
#+end_src

Rotation preserves principal identity while creating new key and binding
identities.  Long-term verification evaluates the historical signing-time
binding, trust policy, revocation observation, algorithm support, and content
commitment rather than rewriting history from the key's current state.

#+begin_src typst
$ rotate(principal, predecessor, successor)
  ==> predecessor.key != successor.key
  and predecessor.binding != successor.binding
  and predecessor.principal = successor.principal $

$ verify-historical(signature)
  ==> retained(signature.content-commitment)
  and retained(signature.key-binding)
  and retained(signature.signing-time-observation)
  and retained(signature.trust-policy)
  and retained(signature.revocation-observation)
  and algorithm-verification-available(signature.algorithm) $

$ migrate-algorithm(old, new)
  ==> old != new
  and new-attestation(old, new)
  and not rewrite(historical-signature(old)) $

$ key-a != key-b or algorithm-a != algorithm-b
  ==> binding-id(principal, key-a, purpose, scope, algorithm-a)
  != binding-id(principal, key-b, purpose, scope, algorithm-b) $
#+end_src

* Central decision

Principal identity, role authority, signing key, encryption key, and trust
anchor are separate objects.

A principal does not become a new principal when a key rotates.

Possession of a valid key does not grant governance or action authority by
itself.

* Identity roles

At least the following identities remain distinct:

- =PrincipalIdentity=;
- =CredentialIdentity=;
- =SigningKeyIdentity=;
- =EncryptionKeyIdentity=;
- =KeyBindingIdentity=;
- =RoleDelegationIdentity=;
- =TrustAnchorIdentity=;
- =AlgorithmIdentity=.

A key is a credential bound to a principal for an exact purpose, scope, and
validity interval.

Cryptographic identity must not collapse these roles into one mutable current
key record.

* POO semantic ownership

Key and trust policy are represented by ordinary higher-order POO objects.
Native prototype composition defines:

- credential purpose;
- binding scope;
- rotation policy;
- revocation admission;
- compromise uncertainty;
- algorithm acceptance;
- reattestation;
- encryption materialization;
- verification bundles;
- trust-domain federation.

The public extension interface remains POO-native. Key status, trust, rotation,
and algorithm selection must not become unstructured macro tokens or mutable
global registries.

* Key binding

A versioned =KeyBinding= records:

- principal identity;
- key identity and public-material commitment;
- signing, encryption, authentication, or other purpose;
- resource and governance scope;
- algorithm and parameter identities;
- validity interval and time authority;
- issuing authority;
- binding version;
- rotation predecessor and successor;
- revocation observation;
- binding receipt identity.

Every signature or encryption materialization references the exact binding
version used.

A valid signature proves origin under a key. It does not prove semantic
correctness, role authority, quorum, or permission to perform a live action.

* Rotation

Key rotation:

1. creates a successor key;
2. creates a successor key binding;
3. activates the binding through authority versioning and compare-and-swap;
4. stops the predecessor from issuing new evidence;
5. preserves predecessor binding, retirement, and revocation evidence;
6. leaves historical receipts bound to their original key identities.

A policy may admit a bounded overlap interval. Every signature still identifies
the exact active binding version.

Historical verification must not depend on one mutable current-public-key
field.

* Revocation roles

Revocation may target:

- one key;
- one principal;
- one role delegation;
- one trust anchor;
- one algorithm;
- one signing or key-management provider.

These targets have different consequences.

Revoking a key does not automatically erase principal identity. Revoking a role
does not automatically invalidate unrelated identity attestations.

Every revocation is an immutable authoritative observation with target, scope,
logical version, time uncertainty, reason, policy, and receipt.

* Historical verification

Historical verification evaluates:

- canonical receipt bytes and content digest;
- signature algorithm and parameters;
- signing key identity;
- principal and purpose binding;
- signing-time evidence;
- binding validity at signing time;
- revocation ordering and uncertainty;
- trust-anchor and delegation evidence;
- current verification policy.

Verification result roles remain distinct:

- cryptographically valid;
- historically admitted under the recorded policy;
- currently admissible;
- mathematically verifiable but no longer trusted;
- unverifiable because required witnesses are unavailable.

A single =valid?= predicate must not collapse these roles.

* Compromise uncertainty

When a compromise boundary is authoritatively known, policy may distinguish
receipts before and after that boundary.

When compromise onset is unknown, policy records an uncertainty interval and
may:

- suspend verification;
- widen the distrusted interval;
- reevaluate affected actions;
- revoke related capabilities;
- reduce trust for the complete key lifetime.

The system must not invent a precise compromise time to preserve historical
admission.

Wall-clock claims alone do not resolve revocation ordering without admitted
time and logical-version evidence.

* Algorithm migration and reattestation

Algorithm migration does not rewrite an old receipt or replace its original
signature.

A new =ReattestationReceipt= records:

- old evidence digest;
- old signature and verification-bundle identities;
- verification result at migration time;
- new algorithm and key-binding identities;
- new trust policy;
- reattestation time evidence;
- new signature and receipt identities.

Reattestation proves that the old evidence was verified at the reattestation
boundary. It does not claim that the old evidence was originally signed with
the new algorithm.

Periodic checkpoint reattestation may preserve a current verification path
while retaining the original evidence chain.

* Signing and encryption separation

Signing keys prove origin under a binding.

Encryption keys control payload readability.

Key-wrapping keys control key materialization.

These keys have separate identities, scopes, rotation, revocation, and
destruction evidence.

Signing-key rotation must not silently rotate payload encryption.
Encryption-key rotation must not change receipt authorship.

* Encryption materialization lifecycle

Rewrapping a data key changes key materialization evidence without necessarily
changing ciphertext or plaintext semantic identity.

Re-encryption creates:

- a new ciphertext materialization identity;
- new encryption-key binding;
- transformation receipt;
- content-integrity verification.

When the admitted plaintext commitment is unchanged, the underlying evidence
semantic identity may remain unchanged.

Key destruction enters the evidence-erasure closure. It requires proof covering
key copies, plaintext replicas, caches, backups, and pending recovery paths.

* Threshold signatures and governance quorum

A threshold signature may prove participation by a cryptographic key-sharing
scheme.

It does not automatically prove governance quorum.

Governance quorum additionally requires independently verifiable:

- principal decisions;
- role and delegation evidence;
- evidence projections;
- proposal binding;
- independence domains;
- decision timing and revocation.

Threshold cryptography supports governance evidence only when these decision
contracts are preserved.

* Trust anchors and domains

Trust anchors are scoped.

A POO trust policy declares:

- trust domain;
- accepted purpose;
- resource scope;
- delegation rules;
- time and revocation requirements;
- admitted algorithms;
- federation evidence.

A cache-signing key in one domain does not authorize production governance in
another domain.

Cross-domain federation requires an explicit trust relation and evidence. It
must not emerge from a shared key format or certificate parser.

* Verification bundles

A long-term =VerificationBundle= may contain:

- original receipt bytes or commitment;
- signature;
- key-binding chain;
- role and delegation evidence;
- trust-anchor identity;
- signing-time evidence;
- revocation snapshot;
- algorithm and verifier schemas;
- disclosure projection;
- checkpoint and reattestation receipts.

The bundle declares its verification and replay level.

Absence of an online revocation service must not be interpreted as evidence
that no revocation occurred.

* Authority boundary

Signature verification does not:

- issue a capability;
- satisfy governance quorum by itself;
- bypass resource compare-and-swap;
- bypass fencing;
- prove payload semantic correctness;
- prove signer authority for a live action.

The complete authority chain remains:

#+begin_example
principal authentication
-> key binding
-> signed decision or evidence
-> role and quorum proof
-> scoped capability issuance
-> versioned action
-> action receipt
#+end_example

* Failure taxonomy

The implementation must distinguish at least:

- principal and key identity collapsed;
- key binding invalid;
- binding purpose mismatch;
- binding scope mismatch;
- signature cryptographically invalid;
- signing-time evidence incomplete;
- revocation ordering unresolved;
- compromise onset uncertain;
- historical policy admitted but current policy rejected;
- verification witness unavailable;
- algorithm no longer admitted;
- reattestation incomplete;
- encryption and signing roles confused;
- re-encryption integrity mismatch;
- key erasure closure incomplete;
- threshold signature mistaken for governance quorum;
- trust-domain federation missing;
- verification bundle incomplete.

* Required invariants

1. Principal identity remains stable across key rotation.
2. Key, role, trust anchor, and algorithm identities remain separate.
3. Every signature references an exact versioned key binding.
4. Rotation preserves original historical receipt bindings.
5. Revocation targets and scopes remain explicit.
6. Historical and current admissibility remain distinct.
7. Compromise uncertainty is represented rather than invented away.
8. Reattestation adds a new verification edge without rewriting history.
9. Signing, encryption, and key-wrapping lifecycles remain separate.
10. Re-encryption creates a new ciphertext materialization receipt.
11. Threshold signatures do not automatically prove governance quorum.
12. Trust anchors remain purpose and domain scoped.
13. Verification bundles declare exact witness and replay capability.
14. Signature verification never grants live action authority.
15. Public trust and key-policy extension remains POO-native.
